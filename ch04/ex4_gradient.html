Potential Gradient

Over a short step ΔL the potential changes by ΔV ≐ −E·ΔL = −E ΔL cos θ, so V rises fastest when ΔL points against E (θ = 180°), and that largest rate of rise is |E|. Hence E = −∇V: E is normal to the equipotentials and points toward lower potential (Hayt Section 4.6, Eqs. (21)–(32)). Equations used

ΔV ≐ −E·ΔL = −E ΔL cos θ   (22)
E = −(dV/dN) aN   (24),   E = −∇V   (29)
∇V = ∂V/∂x ax + ∂V/∂y ay + ∂V/∂z az   (30)
∇V = ∂V/∂ρ aρ + (1/ρ) ∂V/∂φ aφ + ∂V/∂z az   (31)
∇V = ∂V/∂r ar + (1/r) ∂V/∂θ aθ + (1/(r sin θ)) ∂V/∂φ aφ   (32)
D = ε0E (free space),   ρv = ∇·D

The gradient of a scalar is a vector: keep the unit vectors. The factors 1/ρ, 1/r and 1/(r sin θ) in (31) and (32) turn a change of angle into a change of distance (ρ dφ, r dθ, r sin θ dφ are the components of dL). Angles are given in degrees; the derivatives are taken with the angle in radians, which (31) and (32) already allow for.
